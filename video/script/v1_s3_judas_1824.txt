In 1824, *four men* from that same party ran for president. There was *Andrew Jackson*, the war hero from Tennessee. There was *John Quincy Adams*, the brilliant, very serious son of former president John Adams. There was *William Crawford* of Georgia, who'd had a stroke and could barely campaign. And there was *Henry Clay* of Kentucky, the {Speaker of the House}, the leader of the House of Representatives.

Jackson won *the most votes*. But to become president, you need a {majority} of electoral votes, meaning more than half, and with four candidates, *nobody got there*. When that happens, the Constitution lets the *House of Representatives* pick the winner from the *top three*. Clay finished fourth, so he was out. But as Speaker, he *ran the room* that would make the choice.

Clay *backed Adams*, and the House *chose Adams*. A few days later, President Adams named Clay his {Secretary of State}, the country's top diplomat. Back then, that job was seen as the *stepping-stone to the presidency*. Four of the first six presidents had held it.

Jackson exploded. He called it the {corrupt bargain}. And he gave Clay a nickname: the *"Judas of the West."* In the Bible, Judas is the follower who *betrayed Jesus* for *thirty pieces of silver*. Jackson wrote that the Judas of the West had closed the deal and would collect his silver. That's not a nickname you get over.

There's *no proof* Adams and Clay actually made a deal. Clay said he picked Adams because they agreed on policy, and because he thought Jackson was a hotheaded soldier with no business running the country. But *what people believed* mattered more than what happened. Jackson went home to Tennessee and started running for the next election *almost immediately*. And by the time that election came, *millions of new people* could vote in it.
