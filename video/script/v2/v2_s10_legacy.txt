So what did the United States actually get for two and a half years of war? Open up the Treaty of Ghent and the answer is: *almost nothing*. It restored what's called {status quo ante bellum}, Latin for "the way things were before the war." No territory changed hands. Remember Calhoun's *four weeks*? The United States never took an inch of Canada. And impressment, the reason sailors like the ones on the Chesapeake were dragged off their own ships? The treaty *doesn't mention it*. Not once.

So why did it feel like a victory? Because the United States had gone toe-to-toe with the most powerful navy on Earth and *survived*. And because the war pushed Congress to build up the country's own economy, with a new *national bank* and tariffs to protect American factories. The years after the war became known as the *Era of Good Feelings*: one dominant political party, a flood of national pride, and a country that felt less like a loose group of states and more like *one nation*.

That pride showed up somewhere you might not expect: *books*. Before the war, British critics dismissed American writers as cheap imitations. Then a New York writer named *Washington Irving*, who had edited a patriotic magazine during the war, published *"Rip Van Winkle"* in 1819. It's about a man who falls asleep in the mountains and wakes up twenty years later to find his country has changed without him. He dozed off a *subject of the King* and woke up a *citizen of the United States*. The story was a hit in Britain too, and Irving became one of the first American writers famous on *both sides of the Atlantic*. American stories were starting to sound like they came from somewhere *new*.

Not everyone got a happy ending. Remember *Tecumseh*? He never saw any of this. He was killed in *1813* at the *Battle of the Thames*, fighting beside the British against, once again, William Henry Harrison. At the peace talks, British negotiators at first demanded a *Native homeland*, a buffer between the United States and Canada. When the Americans refused, Britain *dropped it*. The same war that pulled the states together into one confident nation was, for Tecumseh's confederacy, the *end of the line*.

The war didn't change the *map*. It changed how Americans *saw themselves*. And that mattered a lot more.
