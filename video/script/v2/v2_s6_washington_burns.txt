By the summer of *1814*, the war had dragged on for two years with nothing decided. Then, in August, a British army marched into *Washington, D.C.*

First Lady *Dolley Madison* was still in the White House. Her husband, President *James Madison*, had ridden out to join the army, and he'd told her to gather important papers and be ready to run. On the wall hung a *life-size portrait of George Washington*. In a letter, Dolley wrote that she *refused to leave* until it was safe, and ordered the frame broken so the canvas could be taken out.

But there's another witness. *Paul Jennings* was an enslaved fifteen-year-old who worked in the house. Years later, he wrote what's often called the *first White House memoir*, and he said Dolley had no time to do it herself. The men who actually took the painting down were the doorkeeper, *Jean-Pierre Sioussat*, and the gardener, *Thomas McGraw*. Either way, the portrait was carted off to safety, and it *still hangs in the White House* today.

Hours later, British soldiers walked into the empty house. They sat down at the dinner table Jennings himself had set for the president, *ate the food*, and then set the building *on fire*. They burned the *Capitol* too. For Americans, watching their own capital go up in flames was *humiliating*. It was also about to *backfire* on the British.
